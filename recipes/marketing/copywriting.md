# Recipe: The Barker's Call (Copywriting)

> The words that stop a passerby mid-stride and make them look. Not louder than the next stall —
> clearer. Clarity is the thing that carries across the noise.

## What you're cooking

You have something worth selling — a product, a page, an offer — and the words on it are doing it a
disservice. Maybe they're vague ("streamline your workflow"), maybe they're all about you and not the
person reading, maybe there's just nothing there yet. Either way, a visitor lands, doesn't see
themselves, and leaves.

This recipe writes the copy: a headline that lands your single most important message, a subheadline
that adds the specifics, sections that each carry one idea, and calls-to-action that tell people
exactly what they get when they click. It works for a homepage, a landing page, a pricing page, a
feature page, or an about page.

You don't need to write a word yourself. Paste this card into your AI (Claude Code, Codex, or similar),
tell it which page you're working on and what you sell, and it drafts the copy with you — explaining
each choice so you can push back.

## Before you start

Read **The Mother Stock** first (`recipes/marketing/product-marketing-context.md`) if it exists — it
holds your positioning, your audience, your proof, and your voice, so the AI shouldn't re-ask what's
already written down. Only gather what the Mother Stock doesn't cover:

- **The page's one job.** Which page is this, and what is the single action you want a visitor to take?
- **The audience.** Who is the ideal customer, what problem are they trying to solve, and what
  hesitations do they arrive with? What words do *they* use for the problem?
- **The offer.** What are you selling, what makes it different, what's the transformation, and what
  proof do you have — real numbers, real testimonials, real case studies?
- **The traffic.** Where do visitors come from (an ad, search, an email), and what do they already
  know before they land?

## The method

Six principles carry the whole thing. **Clarity over cleverness** — if you must choose, choose clear.
**Benefits over features** — a feature is what it does; a benefit is what that means for the reader.
**Specificity over vagueness** — not "save time" but "cut your weekly reporting from four hours to
fifteen minutes." **Customer language over company language** — mirror the words real customers use in
reviews and support tickets. **One idea per section** — each section advances a single argument, and
the sections stack into a logical walk down the page. **Honest over sensational** — never invent a
statistic or a testimonial.

The style rules that make sentences land: simple over complex ("use" not "utilize," "help" not
"facilitate"); active over passive ("we generate reports," not "reports are generated"); confident over
qualified (cut "almost," "very," "really"); show the outcome instead of leaning on adverbs. Strip
exclamation points. Strip buzzwords with no substance under them ("innovative," "seamless,"
"cutting-edge").

A few moves that earn attention honestly. **Be direct** — get to the value, don't bury it under
qualifications. **Ask a rhetorical question** that makes readers picture their own situation ("Tired of
chasing approvals?"). **Reach for an analogy** when it makes an abstract thing concrete. **A little wit**
is welcome when it fits the brand and never fogs the meaning.

The shape of a page. **Above the fold:** a headline carrying your single most important message (try
formulas like "{outcome} without {pain point}," "The {category} for {audience}," "Never {bad thing}
again," or a question naming the main pain); a subheadline of one or two sentences adding specificity;
and a primary call-to-action that says what they get — "Start Free Trial" beats "Sign Up." **Then the
core sections, in an order that argues:** social proof to build credibility, the problem to show you
understand them, the solution as three to five real benefits, a how-it-works of three or four steps to
shrink the perceived complexity, objection handling (FAQ, comparison, guarantee), and a final
call-to-action that recaps the value and reverses the risk.

For the calls-to-action themselves, avoid the weak verbs (Submit, Sign Up, Learn More, Click Here). Use
the formula: action verb plus what they get plus a qualifier if needed — "Get the Complete Checklist,"
"See Pricing for My Team." And tune the copy to the page: a homepage serves several audiences and leads
with the broadest value; a landing page holds one message and one CTA and matches the ad that sent them;
a pricing page calms the "which plan is right for me?" nerves; an about page tells why you exist and
still ends with an ask.

When you hand over the draft, give it organized by section, note *why* the load-bearing choices were
made, and offer two or three alternatives for the headline and the main CTA so the human can choose.
Once there's a draft, season it with **Salt to Taste** (the copy-editing card).

## Acceptance Criteria (how you know it worked)

- [ ] The headline states one clear message, and a stranger can say what the page offers after reading
  only the top of it.
- [ ] Every major claim is a benefit tied to an outcome, not a bare feature.
- [ ] At least the headline and primary CTA come with two or three labeled alternatives and a reason for
  each.
- [ ] The primary CTA names what the reader gets — no "Submit," "Sign Up," or "Learn More."
- [ ] Every number, testimonial, and proof point traces to something the human actually provided.
- [ ] There are no exclamation points and no unsupported buzzwords ("seamless," "innovative,"
  "cutting-edge").

## Primary metric

Conversion rate on the page's one primary action — the share of visitors who take the step the page was
built to earn.

## The bright lines (never cross)

- **Never fabricate a statistic, testimonial, or case study.** If the human didn't give you the proof,
  you don't get to claim it.
- **Never choose clever over clear.** A pun that costs comprehension is a loss, not a flourish.
- **Never write company-first.** The reader's problem leads; who you are comes later, if at all.
- **Never bury or soften the ask.** One primary action per page, stated plainly.

---

*A good barker never lies about what's on the table — the whole trick is that they don't have to. Say
the true thing clearly, to the right person, and they stop on their own.*
