# Recipe: The Taste Test (Competitor & Alternative Pages)

> Set your dish beside the others on the table and let the diner taste for themselves — honestly.
> "vs" and "alternative to" pages that win by being genuinely useful, not by trashing the competition.

## What you're cooking

When someone is choosing between you and a rival, they search for exactly that: "[Competitor] alternative,"
"[You] vs [Competitor]." **This recipe builds the pages that meet them there — comparison and alternative
pages that rank for those searches, help evaluators actually decide, and position you fairly beside the
competition.**

The pain it solves is losing high-intent buyers at the last step, either because you have no page for the
comparison they're making or because the page you have is a dishonest hit piece nobody believes. This
recipe produces well-researched pages in four proven formats, backed by a single source of truth for each
competitor so the facts stay consistent and current.

Paste this card into Claude Code or Codex and say "build my comparison pages." The AI acts as an expert in
competitive content and does the research before it writes.

## Before you start

**Read The Mother Stock first** (`recipes/marketing/product-marketing-context.md` — the master
positioning document). It holds your value proposition, differentiation, ideal customer, pricing model,
and — crucially — your competitive landscape split into direct, secondary, and indirect. Draw all of that
from there. Only ask for what's specific to these pages: the common reasons people switch *to* you, any
customer quotes about switching, your pricing next to competitors', and whether you offer migration
support. If no Mother Stock exists, offer to build it first.

## The method

**Four principles hold the whole thing up:**

1. **Honesty builds trust.** Acknowledge competitor strengths, be accurate about your own limitations, and
   never misrepresent a competitor's features. Your reader is comparing — they will verify your claims, and
   one caught exaggeration sinks the page.
2. **Depth over surface.** Go past the feature checklist. Explain *why* a difference matters, with use
   cases and scenarios. Show, don't just tell.
3. **Help them decide.** Different tools fit different needs. Be clear about who you're best for *and* who
   the competitor is best for. Reducing evaluation friction is the job.
4. **Modular content architecture.** Keep competitor facts in one central place so an update propagates to
   every page — a single source of truth per competitor.

**Four page formats, matched to where the searcher is:**

- **[Competitor] alternative (singular)** — for someone actively wanting to switch. Structure: why people
  look for alternatives (validate the pain), a quick positioning of you as the alternative, a detailed
  comparison, who should switch and who shouldn't, a migration path, social proof from switchers, and a CTA.
- **[Competitor] alternatives (plural)** — for someone earlier in the journey, still researching options.
  Structure: why people look, what to look for (a criteria framework), a list of alternatives (you first
  but **include 4-7 real options**), a summary table, a breakdown of each, a recommendation by use case,
  and a CTA. Being genuinely helpful here ranks better and builds more trust than a self-serving list.
- **You vs [Competitor]** — for a direct head-to-head. Structure: a TL;DR of the key differences in 2-3
  sentences, an at-a-glance table, a detailed comparison by category (features, pricing, support, ease of
  use, integrations), who you're best for, who *they're* best for (honestly), testimonials from switchers,
  migration support, and a CTA.
- **[Competitor A] vs [Competitor B]** — for someone comparing two rivals, not you. Structure: an overview
  of both, comparison by category, who each is best for, then you as the third option, a table of all
  three, and a CTA. This captures competitor search traffic and positions you as the knowledgeable guide.

**Sections that make a page earn its ranking:** always open with a **TL;DR** for scanners. Go beyond
tables with **paragraph comparisons** that explain each dimension and when it matters. In the **feature
comparison**, describe how each tool handles a category, list honest strengths and limits, and give a
bottom-line call. In the **pricing comparison**, go tier by tier, name hidden costs, and compute total
cost for a sample team size. In **who it's for**, be explicit about the ideal customer for each option.
In the **migration section**, cover what transfers, what needs reconfiguring, the support you offer, and
quotes from customers who switched.

**Research before you write — this is where the value is.** For each competitor: sign up and actually use
the product, documenting features and limits; capture current pricing and hidden costs; mine reviews on
G2, Capterra, and TrustRadius for recurring praise and complaint themes; talk to customers who switched in
both directions; and study their positioning, their own comparison pages, and their changelog. Store it
all as a **centralized competitor profile** — positioning, all pricing tiers, feature ratings, strengths
and weaknesses, best-for / not-ideal-for, common complaints, and migration notes — so every page draws
from the same facts.

**Keep it current and connected.** Verify pricing and check for major feature changes **quarterly**, update
when a customer flags a competitor change, and do a full refresh **annually**. Link related comparison
pages to each other, link feature pages to the relevant comparisons, and build a hub page linking to all of
it. Consider FAQ schema for questions like "What is the best alternative to [Competitor]?"

## Acceptance Criteria (how you know it worked)

- [ ] **The format matches the search intent** — the right one of the four formats for where the buyer is in their decision.
- [ ] **Every claim is verifiable** — competitor facts trace back to real research (hands-on use, current pricing, review mining), not assumption.
- [ ] **Competitor strengths are named**, and the page states who the competitor is genuinely best for.
- [ ] **A plural "alternatives" page lists 4-7 real options**, not just you and two strawmen.
- [ ] **Facts live in one place** — a centralized competitor profile feeds every page, so a pricing change updates once.
- [ ] **Each page opens with a TL;DR** and ends with a clear CTA and, where relevant, a migration path.

## Primary metric

`trust` — would a skeptical evaluator, fact-checking your page against the competitor's own site, find it
accurate and fair? A comparison page only converts if the reader believes it, and they only believe it if
it's true.

## The bright lines (never cross)

- **Never fabricate competitor claims.** Every stated feature, price, or limitation must come from real
  research. Inventing a competitor's shortcoming is the fastest way to lose the reader and invite a legal
  letter.
- **Never misrepresent your own limitations.** Be honest about what you don't do well. Readers reward candor
  and punish spin.
- **Always name who the competitor is best for.** A page that claims you win every scenario reads as
  propaganda and converts worse than an honest one.
- **Keep the facts fresh.** Stale pricing or a feature the competitor shipped last quarter makes the whole
  page — and your brand — look careless. Honor the quarterly/annual refresh cadence.
