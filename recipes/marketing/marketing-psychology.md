# Recipe: The Craving (Marketing Psychology)

> Why people reach for what they reach for — and how to earn that reach honestly, without ever
> tricking the hand that reaches.

## What you're cooking

You have something worth choosing, and you're trying to understand why people do or don't choose it.
The guessing is exhausting: you change the button color, you rewrite the headline, you drop the price a
few dollars, and you can't tell what actually moved the needle or why. Underneath every one of those
choices is a person deciding, and people decide in patterns that behavioral science has mapped for
decades.

This recipe hands your AI a working library of those patterns — mental models and cognitive biases with
names like *loss aversion*, *social proof*, *anchoring*, and *the paradox of choice* — and teaches it to
diagnose your specific situation with them: which pattern explains why customers stall here, and what a
principled response looks like. You get an explanation you can act on instead of another blind tweak.

You don't need a psychology background. Paste this card into your AI (Claude Code, Codex, or similar),
tell it what behavior you're trying to understand or influence, and it works through the relevant models
with you — naming each one, explaining the human truth underneath, and proposing a move you'd be proud to
have made in the open.

## Before you start

Read **The Mother Stock** first (`recipes/marketing/product-marketing-context.md`) if it exists — it
holds your positioning, your audience, your proof, and your voice, so the AI shouldn't re-ask what's
already written down. Only gather what the Mother Stock doesn't cover:

- **The behavior.** What specific action are you trying to understand or influence — a signup, an upgrade,
  a renewal, a first "aha"?
- **The starting belief.** What does the customer already think and feel before they meet your marketing?
  You cannot move someone from a place you haven't located.
- **The stage.** Where in the journey is this — first awareness, weighing the choice, or the final
  decision? The same model does different work at different stages.
- **The blocker.** What's actually preventing the action right now — friction, doubt, cost, confusion, no
  reason to act today? Name your best guess.
- **The evidence.** Have you tested any of this with real customers, or is it a hypothesis? Say which.

## The method

Start by finding the right problem before reaching for a tactic. A handful of **foundational thinking
models** keep the diagnosis honest: *Jobs to Be Done* (people don't buy a drill, they buy a hole — frame
around the outcome, not the feature); *first principles* and the *5 Whys* (tunnel down to the real cause
instead of copying competitors); *inversion* (ask what would guarantee failure, then prevent each thing);
the *theory of constraints* (find the one bottleneck limiting the whole funnel before optimizing anywhere
else); and the *Pareto principle* (the vital 20% of channels or messages driving 80% of results). *Map is
not territory* — your dashboard and your persona are models of real people, not the people themselves, so
stay in touch with actual customers.

Then reach for the models that explain **how buyers think**. Trust is built with *social proof* (people
follow what others do), *authority* (credentials and expertise earn deference), *reciprocity* (give real
value first and people want to give back), and the *pratfall effect* (admitting a small honest flaw makes
you more likable, not less). Value and ownership shift with the *endowment effect* and *IKEA effect*
(people prize what they own or helped build — free trials and configuration create that feeling) and the
*zero-price effect* (free is not just cheap, it's psychologically different). Movement is governed by
*loss aversion* (a loss hurts about twice as much as the same gain feels good), *present bias* (immediate
benefits beat future ones — "start today" beats "ROI in six months"), *status-quo bias* (change feels
risky, so reduce the friction of switching), and the *paradox of choice* with *Hick's Law* (too many
options paralyze — three tiers beat seven). To finish what's started, lean on the *goal-gradient effect*
and *Zeigarnik effect* (progress bars and open loops pull people to completion).

For **pricing perception**, the same true price can be framed several honest ways: *anchoring* (show the
higher number first so the real one reads as reasonable), *good-better-best* (a middle tier reads fair
between a cheap anchor and a premium ceiling), *mental accounting* ("$1 a day" and "$30 a month" are the
same money in different mental pockets), and *charm vs. rounded pricing* ($497 signals value, $500 signals
premium — match the ending to the promise). The *rule of 100*: under $100, a percentage discount looks
bigger ("20% off"); over $100, the absolute number does ("$100 off").

To turn a diagnosis into a design, three behavior models converge on the same truth. *BJ Fogg*: behavior
happens only when **motivation, ability, and a prompt** land at once — high motivation with a hard task,
or an easy task with no prompt, both go nowhere. *EAST*: make the desired action **Easy, Attractive,
Social, and Timely**. And *activation energy*: shrink the very first step until it's trivial — pre-fill
the form, offer the template, show the quick win. When you propose a move, name the model, state the human
truth under it, and say plainly how you'd apply it in the open.

Two warnings the models themselves teach. *Second-order thinking*: a flash sale lifts revenue today
(first order) and trains customers to wait for discounts (second order) — always ask "and then what?" And
the *cobra effect*: incentives backfire when people optimize the reward instead of the goal (a referral
bonus that attracts fraud, not friends). Test incentive structures before you trust them.

## Acceptance Criteria (how you know it worked)

- [ ] Every recommendation names the specific model behind it (e.g. "this is *loss aversion*") and the
  human truth that makes it work — no unexplained "do this."
- [ ] The diagnosis starts from the real bottleneck (theory of constraints), not the first tactic that
  came to mind.
- [ ] At least one recommendation is framed with *second-order thinking* — the "and then what?" is stated,
  not ignored.
- [ ] Every persuasion move is one you could describe to the customer's face without embarrassment; the
  card flags any that couldn't pass that test.
- [ ] Scarcity, urgency, and social proof are used only where the claim is literally true (real stock
  limits, real deadlines, real customers).
- [ ] The AI distinguishes what's been tested with real customers from what's still a hypothesis, and says
  which is which.

## Primary metric

The conversion rate on the one behavior you set out to influence — measured before and after the change,
so you know the model actually moved a real person and not just a theory.

## The bright lines (never cross)

- **Persuasion serves truth, never replaces it.** These models explain how real people weigh a real
  offer. The moment a bias is used to make a false thing feel true, it's manipulation, and this cookbook's
  Care Doctrine fails it. If it only works because the customer is confused, it doesn't count as working.
- **Never manufacture scarcity, urgency, or social proof.** A fake countdown, an invented "only 2 left,"
  a testimonial that never happened — these are lies wearing a psychology costume. Use these levers only
  when the underlying fact is real.
- **Never exploit a vulnerable moment.** Present bias and loss aversion are strongest on people who are
  frightened, grieving, or in crisis. Do not aim them there. Defer to the person's dignity over the
  conversion.
- **Never weaponize a default or a dark pattern.** Pre-selecting the plan most people genuinely want is
  fine; pre-checking a charge someone didn't ask for, or making cancellation a maze, is not.
- **Tell the truth about the evidence.** Don't dress a hunch as a proven principle. If it hasn't been
  tested with your customers, say so.

---

*Every one of these patterns is just a description of how a real person, on a real day, decides what to
reach for. Understand the reaching and you can meet it with something genuinely worth reaching for — which
is the only kind of marketing that survives the customer actually getting what they were sold.*
