# Recipe: Salt to Taste (Copy-Editing)

> The dish is cooked; now it needs seasoning. You're not starting over — you're tasting, adjusting, and
> tasting again until it's tight, clear, and clean.

## What you're cooking

You already have copy. It's fine. But "fine" doesn't convert — it's got a vague line here, a feature
with no benefit there, a claim nobody would believe, a wall of text that scares off the reader. The
temptation is to rewrite the whole thing, and that usually makes it worse, because you lose the voice
that was working.

This recipe does the opposite of a rewrite. It runs your copy through a series of focused passes — one
concern at a time — enhancing what's there instead of replacing it. You end with copy that says the
same thing it always meant to say, just sharper, more concrete, and easier to act on.

Paste this card into your AI, give it the copy and what it's supposed to achieve, and it edits with you
pass by pass — showing what it found, why it matters, and the specific fix, while leaving the final call
to you.

## Before you start

Read **The Mother Stock** first (`recipes/marketing/product-marketing-context.md`) if it exists — the
brand voice and the real customer language live there, and edits should pull toward them. Only ask for
what it doesn't cover:

- **The goal of this copy** — awareness, conversion, or retention?
- **The action** the reader should take.
- **Known trouble spots** the human already suspects.
- **The proof available** — the real numbers, names, and results you're allowed to cite.

Then do the thing every good editor does first: read the whole piece through once *without touching it.*

## The method

The core belief: editing is enhancing, not rewriting. Don't change the core message; keep the author's
voice. Each edit needs a reason. Many small focused passes beat one big unfocused one.

Run the **Seven Sweeps** in order. After each sweep, loop back and confirm the earlier sweeps didn't
break.

1. **Clarity.** Can the reader understand it? Hunt confusing sentences, unclear pronouns, jargon,
   ambiguity, missing context. The usual killers: sentences doing too much, abstract where it should be
   concrete, assuming knowledge the reader doesn't have. Keep the Rule of One (one idea per section) and
   the You Rule (the copy talks to the reader).
2. **Voice and tone.** Does it sound like one consistent person? Watch for copy that starts casual and
   drifts corporate, "we" turning into "the company," humor that flickers on and off by accident. Read
   it aloud to hear the seams.
3. **So What.** After every claim, literally ask "so what?" If there's no deeper benefit answering it,
   add the "which means…" bridge. "Our platform uses AI-powered analytics" earns a *so what?* until it
   becomes "…surface insights you'd miss manually, so you decide in half the time."
4. **Prove It.** Is every claim backed? Flag "trusted by thousands" (which thousands?), "industry-
   leading" (says who?), "customers love us" (show them saying it). Either attach real proof —
   testimonials with names, specific numbers, guarantees — or soften the claim to what's true.
5. **Specificity.** Trade vague for concrete. "Save time" becomes "save four hours a week"; "many
   customers" becomes "2,847 teams"; "fast results" becomes "results in 14 days." When a line can't be
   made specific, it's usually filler — cut it.
6. **Heightened Emotion.** Does it make the reader feel anything? Flat, purely-informational copy
   doesn't move people. Paint the "before" state, use a micro-story, ask a reflective question — while
   staying honest. Emotion serves the message; it is not manipulation.
7. **Zero Risk.** Near every call-to-action, remove the reasons to hesitate. List every objection, then
   check the copy answers each: guarantee, free trial, "no credit card required," "cancel anytime," a
   trust signal, a crystal-clear next step.

When there isn't time for all seven, run the **quick passes.** Cut weak intensifiers (very, really,
extremely) and filler (just, actually, basically); "in order to" becomes "to." Swap weak words for
strong: utilize→use, leverage→use, facilitate→help, implement→set up, innovative→new, robust→strong,
seamless→smooth, cutting-edge→modern. Prefer active voice; turn noun-lumps back into verbs ("make a
decision"→"decide"). At the sentence level: one idea each, vary the length, front-load what matters,
keep most under 25 words. At the paragraph level: one topic each, two to four sentences for the web,
strong opening lines, and white space so it can be skimmed.

Work it collaboratively: run a sweep, present findings with reasons, recommend specific edits, hand
control back to the author to accept, then re-check the earlier sweeps. Repeat until a full sweep turns
up nothing new.

## Acceptance Criteria (how you know it worked)

- [ ] The core message is unchanged — a before/after read shows the same promise, said better.
- [ ] Each of the seven sweeps was run and reported with concrete findings, not a vague "looks good."
- [ ] Every surviving claim either carries real proof or has been softened to what's true.
- [ ] Vague words were replaced with numbers, timeframes, or examples wherever the fact existed to do so.
- [ ] Near every CTA, at least one hesitation is answered with a trust signal or risk reversal.
- [ ] The edited copy reads cleanly aloud, in one consistent voice.

## Primary metric

Reader comprehension on one pass — a fresh reader can state what the copy offers and what to do next
after reading it exactly once.

## The bright lines (never cross)

- **Never invent proof to satisfy the Prove It sweep.** If the evidence doesn't exist, soften the claim
  instead.
- **Never rewrite the author's voice out of it.** You enhance the message; you don't replace the person.
- **Never manufacture emotion by distorting the truth.** Feeling must ride on something real.
- **Never call it done mid-process.** Stop only when a complete sweep finds nothing new to fix.

*Seasoning is patience: a pinch, a taste, a pinch again. The point was never to cook a different dish —
it was to make the one you have taste like exactly what it is.*
