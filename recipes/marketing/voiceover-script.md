# Recipe: The Table Read (Voiceover Script)

> Thirty to fifty words, written for the ear. A script that sounds like a person let you
> in on something — not a robot reading a billboard.

## What you're cooking

Your reel needs a voice. Not a wall of copy read fast, but a handful of words delivered
with the timing and warmth of a real person. The trap is writing for the eye: sentences
that look fine on the page and sound stiff, rushed, or robotic the moment a machine reads
them aloud.

This recipe produces a **spoken voiceover script** — 30 to 50 words for a 15-second reel —
plus all the production direction that makes it *sound human* through a text-to-speech
system (TTS — the software that turns written words into a spoken voice, like Microsoft
Edge TTS, Google Cloud TTS, or ElevenLabs). Spoken language isn't written language: this
is the brand's voice, but contracted, conversational, and tuned to the breath.

Paste this card into Claude Code or Codex with your brand and your reel's timing, and say
"write the voiceover." You'll get voice casting, specific TTS voices to try, the script
marked up with pauses and emphasis, a timing budget, pronunciation fixes, and tone
direction.

## Before you start

The AI should gather context before writing the script:

- **The Mother Stock** — read `recipes/marketing/product-marketing-context.md` if it
  exists: brand voice and tone above all, plus audience and positioning.
- **The Plating Guide** — read the creative brief at `recipes/marketing/creative-brief.md`
  if it exists, for the personality summary.
- **The landing page copy**, if available — its hook and value props are your raw
  material.
- **The storyboard** from The Sizzle Reel, if it exists — so the voiceover's timing lines
  up with the scene beats and the closing word lands on the final scene, not too early.
- Only then ask for anything missing. Don't re-ask what the documents cover.

## The method

Write for the ear, not the eye. **Read every line aloud** — if you stumble, the voice
will too. Cut subordinate clauses, prefer contractions ("you're," not "you are"), and
dodge tongue-twisting sibilance ("six superior solutions"). Build the output in order:

**Voice casting** — three to five bullets specific enough that someone can pick a voice in
under 30 seconds: gender presentation, age range, vocal character (warm and dry; breathy
and intimate; gravelly and authoritative), accent or region, and two or three reference
voices that capture the energy ("Phoebe Waller-Bridge meets a yoga teacher").

**Recommended TTS voices** — name one or two concrete voice options for whichever systems
apply (for example, specific Microsoft Edge or Google voice IDs, or a described profile to
look for in ElevenLabs), so the human isn't left guessing.

**The script, with delivery notation** — the spoken text marked up with cues the TTS or a
voice actor can follow. A small, consistent notation carries it: a bracketed "pause" for a
roughly quarter-second micro-pause; a bracketed "breath" for a natural half-second inhale;
a short em-dash beat for a clipped ~150-millisecond hitch; asterisks around a word to
stress it; a parenthetical tone note at the start of a clause; and sparing ALL CAPS for
sustained emphasis. Shown as quoted prose, a line might read: *(warm, like you're letting
them in on a secret) The 3pm crash? [pause] Skip it.*

**Word and timing budget** — keep the spoken text to **28 to 42 words**. A 15-second reel
holds that comfortably at a conversational ~150 words per minute; go over and the TTS has
to rush, which kills the voice. Shape it in three beats: an open of about 3–5 seconds
(8–12 words), a middle of about 6–8 seconds (16–22 words), and a close of about 2–3
seconds (4–8 words). Name the one line that's the "hookable" sound bite — the phrase that
could stand alone as social audio.

**Pronunciation notes** — only if needed: brand or product terms TTS tends to mangle,
fixed with a plain rewrite or a phonetic spelling ("Bloom & Root" written as "Bloom and
Root"; "ashwagandha" as "ash-wuh-GAHN-duh").

**Tone direction** — two or three sentences on *how* to perform the read, not what the
brand is: "Read this like you're talking to a friend who's had a rough week — not selling,
just letting them in on something good. Don't smile through it; land each line and let it
breathe."

Two more habits: **earn the silence** — 38 words in 15 seconds leaves four or five seconds
for breath, music, or a visual moment, so resist filling every gap — and go easy on hard
calls-to-action. For premium brands, "Find your blend" or "Try it once" lands warmer than
"Buy now." And leave the music to The House Band; here, just note whether the voice sits
*over* music or in a silent beat.

## Acceptance Criteria (how you know it worked)

- [ ] The spoken script is between 28 and 42 words — counted, not estimated.
- [ ] It follows the open / middle / close beat structure with the timings noted.
- [ ] Delivery notation (pauses, breaths, emphasis, tone cues) is present and consistent.
- [ ] The script reads cleanly aloud with no stumbles or sibilance pile-ups.
- [ ] One line is flagged as the standalone hookable sound bite.
- [ ] Any tricky brand or product term has a pronunciation fix.

## Primary metric

`sounds_human` — read aloud (or run through the named TTS voice), does it sound like a
person letting you in on something, rather than a machine reading a sign? Yes wins.

## The bright lines (never cross)

- **28–42 spoken words.** Over the ceiling forces the read to rush and breaks the voice.
- **Write for the ear.** Contractions, no clause pile-ups, no sibilance clusters — every
  line read aloud first.
- **Earn the silence.** Don't fill every second; leave room for breath and music.
- **Match the visual pace.** The closing word lands near the final scene, not early.
- **Don't write the music.** That's The House Band's job — only note over-music or silent.

---

*A voice is intimacy at scale. Give the machine the words, the breaths, and the feeling to
carry them — and thirty seconds of talking becomes someone worth listening to.*
