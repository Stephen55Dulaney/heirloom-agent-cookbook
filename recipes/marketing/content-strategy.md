# Recipe: The Menu Plan (Content Strategy)

> Before you cook a single dish, you decide the menu: which pieces to make, in what order, and why each
> one earns a place on the board. A stall without a plan just cooks whatever's on hand.

## What you're cooking

You know you should be "making content," but the blank page is paralyzing. Which topics? In what order?
Will any of it actually reach the people who'd buy from you? Most content programs fail here — not in
the writing, but in choosing the wrong things to write.

This recipe produces the plan: three to five content pillars your brand can own, the clusters of
supporting pieces under each, a prioritized list of what to make first, and the reason each topic earns
its slot — backed by what your real customers ask and search for. It's the map that turns "make
content" into a specific, ordered to-do list.

Paste this card into your AI, tell it what you sell and who for, hand it any research you have (call
transcripts, keyword exports, survey answers), and it builds the plan with you.

## Before you start

Read **The Mother Stock** first (`recipes/marketing/product-marketing-context.md`) if it exists — it
holds the positioning, the ideal customer, and the problems you solve, so the AI shouldn't re-ask them.
Only gather what it doesn't cover:

- **Business context.** What the company does, who the ideal customer is, and the primary goal for
  content — traffic, leads, brand awareness, or thought leadership.
- **Customer research.** What questions people ask before buying, what objections come up on sales
  calls, what repeats in support tickets, and the exact words customers use for their problems.
- **Current state.** What content already exists and works, and what resources you have (writers, time,
  formats you can produce).
- **The landscape.** Main competitors and the visible gaps in what they publish.

## The method

The organizing law: **every piece must be searchable, shareable, or both — and you prioritize in that
order, because search traffic is the foundation.** Searchable content captures demand that already
exists (people actively looking for an answer). Shareable content creates demand (an idea people pass
around). For searchable pieces: target a specific question, match the searcher's intent exactly, put the
keyword in the title and first heading, and cover the topic completely. For shareable pieces: lead with
a novel insight, original data, or a counterintuitive take, and tell it so people feel something and
look smart passing it on.

**Build around pillars.** Choose three to five core topics your brand will own; each pillar spawns a
cluster of related pieces that interlink. Find your pillars four ways: product-led (what problems your
product solves), audience-led (what your ideal customer needs to learn), search-led (what has volume in
your space), competitor-led (what rivals rank for). A good pillar aligns with your product, matches what
the audience cares about, has search or social interest, and is broad enough to spawn many subtopics.
(Most pieces can just live under `/blog` with good internal linking; reserve elaborate hub-and-spoke
URL structures for a few big, layered topics.)

**Map topics to the buyer's journey** with proven keyword modifiers. Awareness: "what is," "how to,"
"guide to." Consideration: "best," "top," "vs," "alternatives." Decision: "pricing," "reviews," "demo,"
"trial." Implementation: "templates," "examples," "tutorial," "setup." A healthy plan has pieces at each
stage, not just the top.

**Mine the real sources** for ideas instead of guessing. Keyword exports (group into clusters, tag the
buyer stage, spot low-competition quick wins and gaps competitors own that you don't). Call transcripts
(questions become FAQs, pain points become titles in the customer's own words, objections become pieces
that answer them before they're raised). Survey responses (a theme mentioned by 30% or more is high
priority). Forum research (search Reddit and Quora for the topic; harvest the recurring questions,
misconceptions, and debates). Competitor blogs (what they cover well, and the angles they miss). And
your own sales and support teams (the objections and questions they hear daily).

**Then prioritize honestly** by scoring each idea on four weighted factors: Customer Impact (40% — how
often it came up, how many customers face it, how charged the pain is), Content-Market Fit (30% — does
it align with what your product solves and lead naturally to interest), Search Potential (20% — volume,
competition, trend direction), and Resource Requirements (10% — do you have the expertise and assets).
Rank the ideas by total score; that ranked list *is* the order you cook in.

Deliver the plan as: the three-to-five pillars with their rationale and clusters; a prioritized list of
pieces, each tagged searchable/shareable/both, with its content type, target keyword, buyer stage, and
the customer-research reason it made the cut; and a simple map showing how the pieces link together.

## Acceptance Criteria (how you know it worked)

- [ ] The plan names three to five pillars, each with a stated reason it connects to the product and the
  audience.
- [ ] Every recommended piece is labeled searchable, shareable, or both.
- [ ] The recommended pieces span more than one buyer stage — not only top-of-funnel "what is" posts.
- [ ] Each priority topic cites the customer research (a real question, ticket, or search) behind it.
- [ ] Ideas are ranked by the four-factor score, so the human knows what to make first, not just a flat
  list.
- [ ] A cluster map shows how the pieces interlink, rather than a pile of unrelated titles.

## Primary metric

Share of published pieces that reach their intended audience — organic search traffic for searchable
pieces, shares and reach for shareable ones — measured against the plan's stated goal.

## The bright lines (never cross)

- **Never recommend a topic with no customer or search evidence behind it.** Guesses don't earn a slot.
- **Never let shareable ambition crowd out the searchable foundation.** Search comes first for a reason.
- **Never invent search volumes or survey percentages.** Use the human's real data, or say you're
  estimating.
- **Never plan more than the stated resources can actually produce and maintain.** A menu you can't cook
  is a menu that rots.

---

*A good menu is a series of promises the kitchen can keep. Plan for the crowd you actually have, in the
order that feeds them, and every dish you make afterward already knows why it exists.*
