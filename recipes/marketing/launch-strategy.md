# Recipe: Opening Day (Launch Strategy)

> A launch isn't a single dinner service — it's a soft opening, a few friends-and-family nights, then
> the doors flung wide. Build momentum in stages so the crowd arrives when the kitchen is ready.

## What you're cooking

The myth of the launch is the one big day: you flip a switch, the world notices, you're a success. Real
launches don't work that way. **This recipe produces a phased launch plan — for a new product, a feature,
a Product Hunt debut, or a full go-to-market — that gets your product into hands early, learns from real
feedback, and makes a splash at every stage instead of gambling everything on one moment.**

The pain it solves is the anticlimax: the "big launch" that lands to silence because nobody knew it was
coming and there was no audience waiting. This recipe gives you a channel strategy, a five-stage rollout,
a Product Hunt playbook if you want one, and a checklist for before, during, and after the day.

Paste this card into Claude Code or Codex and say "plan my launch." The AI acts as a SaaS launch expert
and builds the plan around your actual audience, timeline, and history.

## Before you start

**Read The Mother Stock first** (`recipes/marketing/product-marketing-context.md` — the master
positioning document). If it exists, it holds your audience, your differentiation, and your proof points —
don't re-ask for those. Only gather what's specific to this launch: what you're launching (new product,
major feature, or minor update), your current audience size and engagement, which owned channels you
already have, your timeline, whether you've launched before and what worked, and whether Product Hunt is
in play. If no Mother Stock exists, offer to build it first.

## The method

**The ORB framework — three kinds of channel, all funneling to what you own.**

- **Owned channels** — email list, blog, podcast, branded community, your product itself. You control
  these; they compound over time and don't depend on an algorithm. Start with 1-2 based on your audience:
  a blog if your industry lacks good content, email if people want direct updates, a community if
  engagement is the point. *Superhuman* built demand through an invite-only waitlist and a 30-minute live
  onboarding demo for every user — exclusivity and word-of-mouth, all through owned relationships.
- **Rented channels** — social platforms, app stores, YouTube, Reddit. They give visibility but you don't
  control the rules. Pick 1-2 where your audience actually is, and use them to *drive traffic to owned
  channels* — never as the whole strategy. *Notion* went viral on Twitter, YouTube, and Reddit but funneled
  every viral post into signups and email onboarding.
- **Borrowed channels** — guest content, collaborations, speaking, influencer partnerships. You tap
  someone else's audience to shortcut the hardest part, getting noticed. Be proactive: list the leaders
  your audience follows, pitch win-win collaborations, use audience-overlap tools, set up referral
  incentives. *TRMNL* sent a free e-ink display to a YouTuber (no paid sponsorship) and a single 500K-view
  review drove $500K+ in sales. Borrowed attention only pays off if you convert it into owned relationships.

**The five-phase rollout.** Momentum is built, not switched on:

1. **Internal launch** — recruit early users one-on-one, collect feedback, iron out major issues. The
   prototype only needs to be demo-able, not production-ready. Goal: validate the core with friendly users.
2. **Alpha launch** — put it in front of external users in a controlled way: a landing page with an
   early-access signup, individual invites, an MVP working in production. Goal: first external validation
   and the start of a waitlist.
3. **Beta launch** — work through the early-access list (some free, some paid), start teasing the problem
   you solve, recruit friends, investors, and influencers to test and share. Goal: buzz plus broader feedback.
4. **Early access launch** — leak details (screenshots, feature GIFs, demos), gather usage data and
   qualitative feedback, run user research with engaged users. Either throttle invites in batches (5-10% at
   a time) or open the whole list under an "early access" framing. Goal: validate at scale.
5. **Full launch** — open self-serve signups, start charging, announce general availability everywhere:
   customer emails, in-app popups, a website banner, a blog post, social posts, and Product Hunt / BetaList
   / Hacker News. Goal: maximum visibility and conversion.

**The Product Hunt playbook (if you use it).** It reaches tech-savvy early adopters and gives a
credibility bump, but it's competitive, short-lived, and rewards preparation — it is not magic.
*Before the day:* build relationships with supporters and communities in advance, optimize the listing
(sharp tagline, polished visuals, short demo video), study successful launches, provide value in
communities before pitching, and prep the team for all-day engagement. *On the day:* treat it as an
all-day event, respond to every comment in real time, spark discussion, rally your existing audience, and
direct traffic back to your site to capture signups. *After:* follow up with everyone who engaged and
convert the traffic into owned relationships. *SavvyCal* reached #2 Product of the Month and *Reform*
reached #1 Product of the Day by doing exactly this.

**After the launch, keep it going.** Set up an onboarding email sequence, fold the announcement into your
roundup email to catch people who missed it, publish comparison pages, update your site with dedicated
sections, and offer an interactive demo. Then plan the *next* launch moment — every feature, integration,
and update is another chance to make a splash. Match the effort to the size: **major** updates get a full
multi-channel campaign, **medium** ones get a targeted email and in-app banner, **minor** ones just get a
changelog entry that quietly signals the product is alive and improving.

## Acceptance Criteria (how you know it worked)

- [ ] **Channels are chosen and named** across owned, rented, and borrowed — with 1-2 owned channels as the destination everything funnels toward.
- [ ] **The plan is phased**, not a single day — each of the relevant five phases has concrete actions and a stated goal.
- [ ] **There's a waitlist / early-access mechanism** in place before the full launch, not bolted on after.
- [ ] **If Product Hunt is used**, there's a before / during / after plan, not just "post it and hope."
- [ ] **Post-launch is planned** — an onboarding sequence and at least one momentum-keeping tactic exist beyond launch day.
- [ ] **Update tiers are set** — the plan says which future updates get full campaigns vs. a changelog line.

## Primary metric

`conversion-to-owned` — how much of the launch's attention lands in a channel you own (an email signup, a
community join) rather than evaporating on a rented platform. Borrowed and rented attention is only worth
what you capture from it.

## The bright lines (never cross)

- **Never fabricate traction or social proof.** Waitlist numbers, testimonials, and "as seen on" claims go
  in only if they're real. A launch built on invented momentum collapses on contact with the crowd.
- **Never rely on a rented channel as the whole strategy.** Algorithms shift and rules change; if all your
  audience lives somewhere you don't control, one policy change can erase it.
- **Don't misrepresent the product's stage.** If it's a beta, call it a beta. Overselling readiness on
  launch day is how you turn first impressions into refund requests.
- **Match effort to substance.** Don't run a full-fanfare campaign for a bug fix — crying "launch" too
  often trains your audience to ignore you.
