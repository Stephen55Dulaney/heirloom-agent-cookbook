# Recipe: The Cold Knock (Cold Email)

> First contact with a stranger who never asked to hear from you. The trick isn't volume or charm — it's
> knocking like a neighbor, not a salesman, and having one true reason to be at the door.

## What you're cooking

You want to reach people who don't know you exist — a decision-maker at a company you'd love to work
with — and turn a cold inbox into a reply. Most cold email fails because it reeks of a template: "I hope
this email finds you well," a paragraph about your company, a request for thirty minutes. It gets
deleted in a second.

This recipe writes cold outreach that sounds like a sharp, thoughtful human noticed something real and
reached out — plus the follow-up sequence that earns a reply when the first note doesn't. Short,
specific, about the reader's world, with one easy thing to say yes to.

Paste this card into your AI, tell it who you're writing to and why them specifically, and it drafts the
email and follow-ups. Give it a real signal (they're hiring, they posted something, they raised
funding) and it gets much sharper.

## Before you start

Read **The Mother Stock** first (`recipes/marketing/product-marketing-context.md`) if it exists — your
value proposition and proof points are there. Only ask for what it doesn't cover:

- **Who you're writing to** — role, company, and why *them* specifically.
- **What you want** — the outcome (a reply, a meeting, an intro, a demo).
- **The value** — the specific problem you solve for people like them.
- **Your proof** — a real result, case study, or credibility signal.
- **Research signals** — funding, hiring, a LinkedIn post, company news, a tech-stack change.

Work with whatever the human gives you. A strong signal plus a clear value proposition is enough to
write; don't block on missing inputs — use what's there and note what would make it stronger.

## The method

The target voice is **a smart colleague who noticed something relevant and is sharing it** —
conversational but not sloppy, confident but not pushy. Five principles hold it together.

**Write like a peer, not a vendor.** Use contractions, read it aloud, and if it sounds like marketing
copy, rewrite it. **Every sentence must earn its place** — cold email is ruthlessly short; if a line
doesn't move the reader toward replying, cut it. The best ones feel like they could have been shorter.
**Personalization must connect to the problem** — if you can delete the personalized opening and the
email still makes sense, the personalization isn't working; the observation should lead naturally into
why you're reaching out. **Lead with their world, not yours** — "you/your" should outweigh "I/we," and
you never open with who you are or what your company does. **One ask, low friction** — an interest-based
question ("Worth exploring?" / "Would this be useful?") beats a meeting request, one CTA per email, easy
to answer in a single line.

**Structure:** there's no single right shape — pick one that fits, or write freeform if it flows.
Shapes that work: Observation → Problem → Proof → Ask ("You're doing X, which usually means Y challenge;
we helped Z with that — interested?"); Question → Value → Ask; Trigger → Insight → Ask ("Congrats on X.
That usually creates Y…"); Story → Bridge → Ask.

**Subject lines:** short, boring, internal-looking — their only job is to get the email opened, not to
sell. Two to four words, lowercase, no punctuation tricks; make it look like a note from a colleague
("reply rates," "hiring ops," "Q2 forecast"). No product pitch, no urgency, no emoji, no first name.

**Follow-up sequences:** three to five emails total, with widening gaps. Each must add something new — a
different angle, fresh proof, a useful resource — never "just checking in." Each should stand alone,
since they may not have read the earlier ones. The breakup email is your last touch; honor it.

**Calibrate to the reader.** C-suite: ultra-brief, peer-level, understated. Mid-level: more specific
value, a touch more detail. Technical: precise, no fluff, respect their intelligence.

Before presenting, gut-check: Does it sound like a human wrote it (read it aloud)? Would *you* reply?
Does every sentence serve the reader, not the sender? Is the personalization tied to the problem? Is
there one clear, low-friction ask?

Steer clear of the tells: "I hope this email finds you well," "My name is X and I work at Y," "I came
across your profile"; jargon like synergy, leverage, circle back, best-in-class, leading provider;
feature dumps (one proof point beats ten features); HTML, images, or multiple links; fake "Re:" or
"Fwd:" subject lines; identical templates with only the first name swapped; asking for a 30-minute call
on the first touch.

## Acceptance Criteria (how you know it worked)

- [ ] Removing the personalized opening breaks the email — the personalization is load-bearing, tied to
  the problem.
- [ ] "You/your" outweighs "I/we," and the email doesn't open with who the sender is.
- [ ] The subject line is two to four lowercase words that look internal, with no pitch or emoji.
- [ ] There's exactly one low-friction ask, answerable in a single line.
- [ ] The follow-up sequence is three to five emails, each adding a genuinely new angle, ending with a
  real breakup email.
- [ ] Read aloud, it passes the "would I reply to this?" test and contains none of the AI/vendor tells.

## Primary metric

Reply rate — the share of recipients who write back (positive or not), the honest signal that the email
earned a human response.

## The bright lines (never cross)

- **Never fabricate a research signal, result, or case study** to manufacture relevance.
- **Never send identical templates with only the first name swapped.** If it isn't specific to them, it
  isn't ready.
- **Never lead with yourself or feature-dump.** Their world first; one proof point, not ten features.
- **Never fake a "Re:" or "Fwd:" subject line** or use any deceptive open trick.
- **Never write a "just checking in" follow-up.** No new value, no send.

---

*A cold knock lands when the person opening the door can tell you actually came to see them. One true
observation, one honest reason, one easy yes — that's the whole craft.*
