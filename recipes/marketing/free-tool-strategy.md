# Recipe: The Free Sample (Free Tool Strategy)

> The stall-keeper who hands you a slice before you buy isn't giving anything away — they're letting the
> thing prove itself. A free tool is that slice: genuinely useful on its own, and an honest taste of what
> you sell.

## What you're cooking

You want people to find you, and you're tired of renting attention — every click bought, every lead paid
for, the moment you stop spending the traffic stops too. There's an older, sturdier move: build something
small and genuinely useful, give it away, and let it draw the right people to you on its own. Marketers
call this *engineering as marketing* — a calculator, a generator, an analyzer that solves one real problem
for free, earns links and search traffic because it's worth referencing, and leads naturally toward the
thing you actually sell.

This recipe helps you find the right tool to build, validate that it's worth building before you build it,
scope it down to a version you can ship, and decide how (and whether) to trade access for an email. Done
well, it's an asset that keeps working for years. Done carelessly, it's a maintenance burden nobody uses —
so this card is as much about saying no to weak ideas as building strong ones.

You don't need to know how to build the tool yourself — that's what your AI is for. Paste this card into
your AI (Claude Code, Codex, or similar), tell it what you sell and who you're trying to reach, and it
works through the idea, the scoring, and the scope with you before a line of the tool gets built.

## Before you start

Read **The Mother Stock** first (`recipes/marketing/product-marketing-context.md`) if it exists — it holds
your core product, audience, and the problems they have, so the AI shouldn't re-ask what's already written
down. Only gather what the Mother Stock doesn't cover:

- **The goal.** What is this tool primarily for — generating leads, earning search traffic, building
  brand awareness, or educating people about the problem you solve? Pick the main one; it changes
  everything downstream.
- **The audience's workarounds.** What clumsy manual process (a spreadsheet, a back-of-envelope
  calculation, a thing they Google repeatedly) does your audience use today because no good tool exists?
- **The resources.** Who can build and, crucially, *maintain* it? A tool nobody tends slowly rots. Is
  there budget to promote it once it's live?

## The method

Four principles decide whether an idea is worth anything. **Solve a real problem** — the tool has to be
genuinely useful even to someone who never buys your product. **Stay adjacent to your core product** — it
should sit next to what you sell so there's a natural path from tool to purchase, and using it teaches the
problem you solve. **Keep it simple and focused** — it does one thing well, with low friction and
immediate value. **Make sure it's worth the investment** — the lead value times the leads you expect has
to beat the cost to build plus the cost to maintain, forever, not just at launch.

To find candidates, start from pain, not from cleverness. What does your audience *Google* repeatedly?
What manual, tedious process could a tool erase? What do they need to figure out *before* they can buy
from you (an assessment, a plan, a comparison)? What information do they wish they had and can't easily
get (a benchmark, a rate, a score)? The common shapes: **calculators** for decisions involving numbers,
**generators** for making something quickly, **analyzers/auditors** for grading existing work,
**testers/validators** for checking if something works, **libraries** for reference material, and
**interactive/educational** pieces for learning. Calculators and analyzers convert especially well because
the output is personal and share-worthy.

Then validate honestly before committing. Is there real **search demand**, and how competitive is it? What
already exists, and can you be *ten times better* rather than marginally different? Does the audience the
tool attracts actually match your buyers, or will you draw a crowd that never converts? And can you
realistically scope an MVP? Put numbers to it with the **evaluation scorecard** — rate each of these 1–5:
search demand exists, audience match to buyers, uniqueness versus what's out there, a natural path to your
product, build feasibility, maintenance burden (inverted — less is better), link-building potential, and
share-worthiness. Roughly: **25 and up** is a strong candidate, **15–24** is promising, **under 15** means
reconsider. The scorecard's real job is to kill weak ideas cheaply, before they cost you months.

On **capturing leads**, choose your gate consciously. Fully gated (email required before use) maximizes
capture but suppresses usage and kills the SEO and link value. Ungated maximizes reach and links but
captures no one directly. Partially gated — the tool works, but the *full report* or *export* asks for an
email — is the common middle ground and usually the right one. Whatever you choose, make the value exchange
explicit ("get your full report"), keep friction minimal (email only), and show a preview of what they'll
get. If your goal is pure SEO and brand rather than leads, ungating entirely is a legitimate choice, not a
failure.

For **SEO**, target the searches people actually type — "[thing] calculator," "[thing] generator," "free
[tool type]" — and support the tool page with explainer content ("how to [use case]," "what is
[concept]"). Free tools earn links precisely because they're useful, unique, and shareable in a way an
ordinary page isn't.

On **build versus buy**: build custom when the concept is unique, core to your brand, and you have the
capacity; use a no-code builder when speed or limited resources matter or you're still testing the idea;
embed an existing white-label tool when something good already exists and it isn't your differentiator.
Whatever the route, ship a real **MVP** — core functionality that does the one thing reliably, essential
UX (clear input, obvious output, works on mobile), and basic lead capture that actually routes the leads
somewhere useful. Deliberately skip, for now: account creation, saving results, advanced features,
pixel-perfect design, and every edge case. Ship the slice, learn, then decide what to add.

## Acceptance Criteria (how you know it worked)

- [ ] The tool is genuinely useful to someone who never buys the main product — usefulness doesn't depend
  on the sales pitch.
- [ ] The idea was scored on the evaluation scorecard before building, and a weak idea (under 15) would
  have been named as such rather than waved through.
- [ ] The tool is adjacent to the core product, with a clear, natural path from using it to buying.
- [ ] The MVP does one thing reliably and works on mobile — no half-finished second feature crowding the
  first.
- [ ] The gating choice is deliberate and stated (fully gated, partial, or ungated), with the tradeoff
  named, not defaulted into.
- [ ] There's an honest answer to "who maintains this?" — a tool with no keeper was flagged, not shipped.

## Primary metric

Qualified leads (or, if the goal is SEO, organic sessions) generated by the tool per month — set against
what it cost to build and keeps costing to maintain, so you can see whether the sample is actually worth
the slice you gave away.

## The bright lines (never cross)

- **The free thing must be genuinely free and genuinely useful.** No fake results behind the gate, no tool
  crippled until you pay, no "free" that's actually a trial in disguise. If the sample isn't real, the
  trust it was meant to build dies on the first bite.
- **Never harvest more than you're honestly trading for.** Ask for the email in a clear exchange for a
  stated deliverable. No hidden data collection, no pre-checked marketing consents, no selling the list.
- **Don't ship a tool you can't maintain.** A broken calculator or a stale analyzer does worse than nothing
  — it teaches people your product is neglected too. If no one will tend it, don't build it.
- **Make the output honest.** If the tool grades, estimates, or analyzes, its numbers must be real and its
  method defensible. A rigged score that always recommends buying you is a lie, not a lead magnet.

---

*A real free sample is a small act of confidence: you hand someone something good and useful, expecting
nothing at the counter, trusting the quality to speak. Build the tool that way — actually helpful, no
strings you're hiding — and the people it draws will already half-believe you before you've said a word.*
