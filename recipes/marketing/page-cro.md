# Recipe: The Storefront (Page CRO)

> A good storefront answers three questions before a stranger has to ask them:
> what is this, is it for me, and what do I do next.

## What you're cooking

You have a page — a homepage, a landing page, a pricing page — and it isn't pulling its weight. People land, look, and leave. The problem is almost never that they hate what you sell. It's that in the five seconds they gave you, they couldn't tell what it was, why it mattered to *them*, or what to do about it.

This recipe turns an AI into a conversion rate optimization expert — CRO, the craft of turning more visitors into customers — and points it at one of your pages. It produces a prioritized list of changes: the quick wins you can ship today, the bigger swings worth planning, and the risky hunches worth testing rather than assuming. Paste this card into Claude Code or Codex, tell it which page, and it will read the page the way a first-time visitor does — impatient, skeptical, scanning.

You don't need to know how any of it is built. You bring the page and the goal; the AI brings the trained eye.

## Before you start

Tell the AI to read **The Mother Stock** first — `recipes/marketing/product-marketing-context.md`, the master positioning document — if it exists. That's where your audience, your promise, and your voice already live; the AI should draw on it and only ask you for what it doesn't already cover.

Then it needs three things about *this* page: what kind of page it is (homepage, landing, pricing, feature, blog), the one action you most want a visitor to take (sign up, book a demo, buy, subscribe), and where the traffic comes from (an ad, a search result, an email, social). That last one matters more than people expect — the page has to echo the promise that brought them.

## The method

Work the page top to bottom, in order of impact. The first item moves the most.

**Value proposition first.** Can a visitor understand what this is and why they should care within five seconds? Say the single most important thing, not everything. Lead with the benefit, not the feature list, and use the customer's words, not company jargon. Vague and too-clever both lose to plain and specific.

**Then the headline.** It should carry that core value, be specific enough to mean something (numbers, timeframes, concrete detail), and match the message of whatever brought the visitor here. Strong patterns: an outcome ("Get X without Y"), a specific number, or honest social proof ("Join 10,000+ teams who...").

**Then the call to action** — the button or link you want clicked. One clear primary action, visible without scrolling, with copy that names the value rather than the mechanic. "Start Free Trial" and "Get My Report" beat "Submit," "Sign Up," and "Learn More." Repeat the primary action at natural decision points, and keep a clean primary-vs-secondary hierarchy so the page never asks two things at once.

**Then scannability.** Most people skim. Can a skimmer get the message from headings and prominent elements alone? Give it white space; make images support the point rather than decorate around it.

**Then trust.** Place social proof near the CTA and right after any big claim: recognizable customer logos, specific attributed testimonials (a photo helps), case-study numbers, review counts, security badges where money or data is involved.

**Then objections.** Name the quiet doubts — price, "will this work for my situation," how hard it is to set up, "what if it doesn't work" — and answer them with FAQs, guarantees, comparisons, or plain honesty about the process.

**Then friction.** Hunt for the small frustrations: too many form fields, unclear next steps, confusing navigation, a rough mobile experience, a slow load.

Each page type has its own emphasis. A **homepage** must orient a cold stranger and serve both the ready-to-buy and the still-researching. A **landing page** should match its traffic source, drop the site navigation, and make its whole argument in one place with a single CTA. A **pricing page** needs a clean plan comparison, a recommended plan marked, and an answer to "which one is right for me?" A **feature page** connects each feature to a benefit and a use case. A **blog post** earns contextual CTAs at natural stopping points.

Deliver the findings in four buckets: Quick Wins, High-Impact Changes, Test Ideas (the hunches that deserve an A/B test instead of a guess), and Copy Alternatives — two or three options for the headline and CTA, each with a reason.

## Acceptance Criteria (how you know it worked)

- [ ] A stranger can state what the page offers and who it's for within five seconds of looking.
- [ ] There is exactly one primary call to action, visible before scrolling, worded as a value not a mechanic.
- [ ] Every recommendation is sorted into Quick Win, High-Impact, or Test Idea — no undifferentiated pile.
- [ ] At least one trust signal sits next to the primary CTA or a major claim.
- [ ] The page's message matches the promise of the traffic source that feeds it.
- [ ] Headline and CTA each come with two or three alternatives and a rationale.

## Primary metric

Conversion rate on the page's one primary action — the share of visitors who take it. Everything else is in service of that number moving up.

## The bright lines (never cross)

- **One page, one primary job.** Don't stack competing calls to action; a page that asks for two things gets neither.
- **Clarity beats cleverness, always.** Never trade a visitor's understanding for a witty line.
- **No invented proof.** Testimonials, logos, and numbers must be real and attributable — a fabricated trust signal is a lie that breaks trust the moment it's caught.
- **Recommend tests for the uncertain.** When a change is a genuine hunch, say "test this," don't dress a guess up as a fact.

---

*A storefront isn't decoration. It's the first honest conversation you get to have with someone in a hurry — so answer their three questions before they have to ask, and make the next step easy to find.*
