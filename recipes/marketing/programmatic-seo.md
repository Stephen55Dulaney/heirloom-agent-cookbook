# Recipe: The Waffle Iron (Programmatic SEO)

> One well-made mold, pressed again and again — each waffle its own, none of them thin. That's the whole
> art: a template good enough that every page it stamps out earns its place.

## What you're cooking

There's a whole shape of search demand you're missing — hundreds or thousands of people searching for
close variations of the same thing ("crm for real estate," "crm for law firms," "crm for agencies").
You can't hand-write a page for each, and you shouldn't. But if you generate them carelessly, you get a
field of near-identical pages that Google penalizes as thin content, and you've made your site worse.

This recipe builds the mold: a page template plus real data, so you can press out many pages that each
carry genuine, page-specific value. It picks the right pattern for what you sell, works out where the
data comes from, designs a template that stays unique page to page, wires the pages into your site so
none are stranded, and sets an indexation plan so only pages worth ranking get pushed forward.

You don't need to build anything by hand. Paste this card into your AI (Claude Code, Codex, or similar),
tell it what you sell and what data you have, and it designs the strategy and the template with you.

## Before you start

Read **The Mother Stock** first (`recipes/marketing/product-marketing-context.md`) if it exists — it
holds your product, audience, and positioning, so the AI shouldn't re-ask what's already written down.
Only gather what it doesn't cover:

- **The opportunity.** What repeating search pattern are you targeting, what are the variables in it, and
  roughly how many real combinations exist? Is there actual search demand behind them, or are you
  inventing pages nobody looks for?
- **The data.** What data will fill each page, and where does it come from — is it yours (proprietary),
  drawn from your product, from your community, licensed, or just public? How does it stay current?
- **The competition.** Who ranks for these terms today, what do their pages look like, and can you
  realistically make something better?

## The method

Six principles hold the whole thing up. **Unique value per page** — every page must offer something
specific to it, not just a swapped-in variable. **Proprietary data wins** — the more the data is truly
yours, the more defensible the pages; the defensibility runs from proprietary (strongest) to
product-derived, to user-generated, to licensed, to plain public data (weakest). **Clean URL structure**
— always use subfolders on your main domain (yoursite.com/templates/resume), never subdomains. **Genuine
intent match** — the page must actually answer what the searcher wanted. **Quality over quantity** —
100 great pages beat 10,000 thin ones. And **avoid the penalties** — no doorway pages, no keyword
stuffing, no duplicate content; there must be real utility for a human.

**Pick the playbook.** There are twelve proven patterns; choose by the asset you already have.
Templates ("resume template") and Examples ("landing page examples") suit design and creative products.
Comparisons ("webflow vs wordpress") and Curation ("best crm software") catch shoppers deciding.
Conversions ("100 kg to lbs") suit utility tools. Locations ("dentists in austin") suit a local
presence. Personas ("crm for real estate") suit a multi-segment audience. Integrations ("slack asana
integration") capture users of other tools. Glossary ("what is pSEO") and Profiles ("stripe ceo") build
topical authority. Directory ("ai copywriting tools") suits aggregators, and Translations open new
language markets. You can layer them — "best coworking spaces in san diego" is Curation plus Locations.
Match the playbook to your strongest data: proprietary data points toward Directories and Profiles;
integrations toward Integrations; local presence toward Locations.

**Then build in five steps.** First, keyword-pattern research: name the repeating structure, its
variables, and the count of combinations, then validate that real search volume exists across them.
Second, data requirements: pin down each source, whether it's first-party or public, and how it refreshes.
Third, template design: a header carrying the target keyword, a genuinely unique intro (not variables
swapped into boilerplate), data-driven sections, links to related pages, and a call-to-action matched to
the searcher's intent — with the uniqueness coming from conditional content and original analysis per
page. Fourth, internal linking on a hub-and-spoke model: a main category page (the hub) linking out to
each generated page (the spokes), spokes cross-linking to related spokes, breadcrumbs, and no orphans.
Fifth, indexation strategy: prioritize the high-volume patterns, mark very thin variations "noindex" so
they don't drag the site down, keep crawl budget in mind on large sites, and split sitemaps by page type.

**Check before you launch and watch after.** Pre-launch: each page offers unique value, matches intent,
and reads well; titles and meta descriptions are unique; headings and schema markup are in place; page
speed is acceptable; every page is linked into the site and in the sitemap with no conflicting noindex.
Post-launch, track indexation rate, rankings, traffic, engagement, and conversion — and watch for the
warning signs: thin-content warnings, ranking drops, manual penalties, and crawl errors. The mistakes
that sink these projects are always the same: thin content from just swapping a city name, two pages
cannibalizing the same keyword, over-generating pages nobody searches for, stale data, and pages built
for Google rather than for people.

Deliver a short strategy document (the opportunity, the plan, the content guidelines) and one page
template (URL structure, title and meta patterns, content outline, and the schema to include).

## Acceptance Criteria (how you know it worked)

- [ ] The chosen playbook is matched to a real asset, and the search demand behind the pattern is
  validated, not assumed.
- [ ] The template produces pages with genuinely unique value per page — not the same body with one
  variable swapped.
- [ ] Every generated page uses a subfolder URL, has a unique title and meta description, and is linked
  into the site via a hub-and-spoke structure with no orphans.
- [ ] Very thin variations are set to noindex, and the indexation plan prioritizes high-volume patterns.
- [ ] A pre-launch quality checklist is run and passes before any page goes live.
- [ ] A post-launch monitoring plan names what to track and the warning signs to watch for.

## Primary metric

Indexed pages that earn organic traffic — the count of generated pages Google both keeps in its index
and sends real visitors to, not the raw number of pages published.

## The bright lines (never cross)

- **Never generate doorway-page spam.** Every page must carry value a human would thank you for; a page
  that exists only to catch a keyword is a penalty waiting to happen.
- **Never ship thin content.** If a page is just a template with one word changed, it doesn't get made.
- **Never use subdomains for these pages.** Subfolders on the main domain, always.
- **Never publish data you can't keep accurate.** Stale or wrong data across thousands of pages erodes
  trust at scale.
- **Never build for Google over the user.** Pages that ignore the human reading them lose both.

---

*The trick was never pressing out more waffles. It was making the iron good enough that you'd be proud to
serve any single one it stamps — then, and only then, pressing it a thousand times.*
